                                                                            Abstract: In many batch studies, the kinetics of adsorption have demonstrated adherence to first-order
                                                                            (Lagergren) or second-order (Ho and McKay) models. Since practical adsorption systems generally operate in
                                                                            the fixed-bed mode, in this work, the first- and second-order kinetic models describing adsorbate uptake are
                                                                            combined with an overall adsorbate material balance to obtain simple analytical expressions for the adsorbate
                                                                            breakthrough curve from a fixed bed of adsorbent. These expressions can correlate some fixed-bed
                                                                            experimental data reported in the literature with an average error of 18%. The equations describing the
                                                                            breakthrough behaviour can be used for the rapid scale-up and design of fixed-bed adsorption columns for
                                                                            removing contaminants from water and wastewater.

                                                                            Keywords: Breakthrough curve, Fixed-bed adsorption, Ho and McKay model, Lagergren model, Water
                                                                            treatment

                                                                            Introduction
                                                                            Modern industrial processes produce a variety of hazardous materials like metal ions, dyes and
                                                                            organic compounds, which have to be removed from waste streams before their discharge into the
                                                                            environment. The removal of such chemicals from liquid and gaseous effluent streams by carbon

                                                                            adsorption, in order to comply with environmental regulations, has been a long-standing practice.
                                                                            Many novel and low-cost adsorbents have been tested for removal of hazardous chemicals [123].
                                                                            Examples of these alternative adsorbents are natural materials like cedar sawdust, popcorn, kudzu,

                                                                            slag created in steel blast furnaces, red mud resulting from aluminium processing, and even carbon
                                                                            developed from waste slurry generated in fertilizer plants [915]. These works have investigated the
                                                                            effects of temperature, initial pH, initial concentration and adsorbent particle size on the adsorption
                                                                            process, and have also presented Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms in order to determine sorbent

                                                                            dosage requirements. In many of these studies, which were conducted in batch systems, the
                                                                            adsorbate uptake was found to obey the first-order model of Lagergren [24] or the second-order
                                                                            model of Ho and McKay [25]. Assuming no mass-transfer effects, Azizian [26] has provided a
                                                                            theoretical justification for the first- and second-order kinetic models in the case of batch
                                                                            adsorption.
                                                                                  Adsorption systems generally operate in the fixed-bed mode for which sophisticated design
                                                                            procedures exist [27]. From a practical point of view, such procedures involve complex
                                                                            mathematical and numerical techniques and presume a high level of technical competence and
                                                                            skill on the part of the practitioner, as well as the associated computational facilities necessary to
                                                                            implement the techniques. These are often lacking in the villages and towns of the countries of Latin
                                                                            America, Africa and Asia, whose populations are increasingly threatened with contamination of
                                                                            their drinking water supplies owing to rapid industrialization and natural factors (e.g. groundwater
                                                                            contamination by geogenic arsenic in West Bengal, India and Bangladesh).
                                                                                  The main objective of this paper is to present simple and alternative methods for the scale-up
                                                                            and design of fixed-bed adsorption columns for removing contaminants (e.g. arsenic, iron,
                                                                            chromium and organic dyes) from water and wastewater, which can be easily implemented without
                                                                            requiring significant expertise or computational facilities. Specifically, the first- and second-order
                                                                            kinetic models describing adsorbate uptake [24, 25], which were originally developed for batch
                                                                            adsorption, are combined with an overall adsorbate material balance in order to derive
                                                                            analytical expressions for the adsorbate breakthrough curve from a fixed bed of adsorbent. These
                                                                            expressions are used to correlate some fixed-bed experimental data that have been reported in the
                                                                            literature.

                                                                            Results and Discussion
                                                                            The models were correlated against experimental data from three separate investigations [13] that
                                                                            involved the adsorption of a component from a liquid or gaseous phase on a solid adsorbent. In
                                                                            order to discriminate between first- and second-order behaviour, the data were first plotted
                                                                            according to Equation (16). If the correlation coefficient of the fit was 0.98, first-order behaviour
                                                                            was presumed; if this was not the case, we fitted the data with the second-order expression given by
                                                                            Equation (21b). Walker and Weatherley [1], who used the beddepthservicetime model to

                                                                                 Metal-ion adsorption is generally pH-dependent [2732]. Since the models presented in this
                                                                            work are concerned with adsorption of a single species, they are unable to account for effects of the
                                                                            solution pH. However, it may be possible to include pH effects in the kinetic approach by
                                                                            developing two-species or three-species models of competitive adsorption. From the point of view
                                                                            of practical application, this can be a simple route to designing fixed-bed adsorption columns for the
                                                                            removal of iron and arsenic, which are often present together from groundwater. As can be seen
                                                                            from Equations (11b) and (21b), only two parameters are needed to describe the breakthrough curve
                                                                            in the kinetic method, both of which can be directly estimated from experimental breakthrough data
                                                                            with no subsidiary experiments (e.g. batch studies for determining sorption equilibria and
                                                                            intraparticle diffusion coefficients) being necessary. While this lumped-parameter approach may
                                                                            not be as technically sophisticated as the distributed-parameter or mechanistic approach practised in
                                                                            the adsorption field [27], it nevertheless has the qualities of harmony and simplicity, and allows one
                                                                            to grasp the totality of the fixed-bed adsorption process.


                                                                            Conclusion
                                                                            First- and second-order kinetic models of adsorbate uptake, which have been used extensively in the
                                                                            literature to correlate results of batch adsorption experiments, were combined with an overall
                                                                            adsorbate material balance to derive analytical expressions for adsorbate breakthrough in a fixed
                                                                            bed of adsorbent. These expressions correlated some convex-shaped experimental breakthrough
                                                                            data, reported in the literature, with an average error of 18%. The simple, analytical expressions for
                                                                            the breakthrough curve presented in this paper can guide the adsorption practitioner in developing
                                                                            rapid scale-up and design methods for fixed-bed adsorption columns in the field from experimental
                                                                            data obtained with a laboratory or pilot column.
